How to read weather radar in Your World Weather Radar

Listen uses your browser's built-in voice.

Radar is the fastest way to answer the question everyone asks when the sky darkens: is it coming here, and how bad is it? This guide walks through what you are actually looking at on a radar map and how to use the radar, alerts and briefings in Your World Weather Radar together.

What radar is showing you

Weather radar sends out pulses of microwave energy and listens for the echo that bounces back off raindrops, snowflakes and hail. In the U.S., the National Weather Service runs a network of about 160 high-resolution Doppler radars called NEXRAD (WSR-88D), operated jointly by NOAA's National Weather Service, the FAA and the U.S. Air Force.

Most radar maps show reflectivity, measured in dBZ. The more energy that comes back, the higher the number and the heavier the precipitation is likely to be.

What the colors mean

  • Light blue or light green (below about 20 dBZ): very light rain, drizzle, light snow, or echoes that aren't weather at all.
  • Green to yellow (about 20–40 dBZ): light to moderate rain.
  • Yellow to orange (about 40–50 dBZ): heavy rain and thunderstorms.
  • Red (about 50–65 dBZ): very heavy rain, possibly small hail.
  • Magenta, purple or white (65+ dBZ): often hail.

These ranges are approximate, and color scales differ between apps. In Your World Weather Radar, rain uses the classic colors and snow is kept light blue, so a winter band is easy to tell apart from a rain shield.

Shapes worth knowing

  • Bow echo: a line of storms bulging outward like an archer's bow. It often brings damaging straight-line winds at the leading edge.
  • Hook echo: a hook-shaped appendage on the back side of a supercell, which can indicate rotation and a possible tornado.
  • Squall line: a long, narrow line of storms that can bring a burst of wind and heavy rain as it passes.

Doppler radar also measures how fast precipitation moves toward or away from the radar. On velocity maps, green usually means motion toward the radar and red means away. A tight pairing of bright green and bright red side by side, called a velocity couplet, can signal rotation.

Use the loop, not a single frame

One radar image tells you where precipitation is right now. The loop tells you where it is going. In the app's live radar you can play the loop back to see how a storm has been moving and growing, and ahead to see where it is heading. Watch two or three cycles: is the line speeding up, bowing out, or falling apart?

From there, add layers when you need them. Lightning and hurricane layers help on stormy days, and the app also offers fire, earthquake and volcano layers, plus aviation, boating and space overlays. Live radar is free; several of the extra layers are part of Premium.

Pair radar with alerts and briefings

Radar shows precipitation, not official warnings. Your World Weather Radar shows National Weather Service alerts as compact cards, and it can send rain and storm notifications in the background, so you don't have to keep the map open.

The AI Briefing tabs turn the forecast into a short summary for each window. The NOW tab is free; the 6–24H and day tabs from 2D to 10D are Premium. Premium users can also download Google's Gemma 3 (1B) model to write briefings on the phone itself, so the forecast never leaves the device and it works offline after the download. When a hurricane is active, Premium adds the NHC forecast track and cone on the map and a HURRICANE briefing tab.

Know radar's limits

  • The radar beam travels in a straight line while the Earth curves away below it, so far from a radar site the beam is high above the ground and can miss low clouds and light precipitation.
  • Mountains can block the beam.
  • Birds, insects, dust and ground clutter can show up as echoes, especially near the radar site.

If the radar looks quiet but the sky doesn't, trust your eyes and check the alerts. Live weather cams in the app (from USGS, NOAA, Windy.com and WebCOOS coastal cams, plus YouTube live streams) are a free way to see conditions for yourself.

A simple storm-day routine

  1. Open the radar and play the loop to see direction and speed.
  2. Turn on the lightning layer if storms are nearby.
  3. Check the alert cards for any watches or warnings for your area.
  4. Read the NOW briefing for a quick summary.
  5. If you'll be out of signal, the free Off-Grid Safety feature can save your last location and text it to emergency contacts, by satellite on phones that support it (carrier rates may apply).

Forecasts and alerts in the app are informational only. They aren't official emergency, marine or aviation advice. Always follow the National Weather Service and local officials.
